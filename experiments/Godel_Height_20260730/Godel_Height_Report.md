<h1>The Gödel‑Height Experiment × the Generative‑vs‑Frame‑Resolvable Discriminator</h1><p><em>A model and a simulation, run together, on the question: as a self‑referential system climbs its stack of "rules about the rules," do its paradoxes dissolve to a fixed point — and if a remainder survives, is that remainder a defect to be crushed or a load‑bearing, generative feature to be protected? And what sets the height at which climbing should stop — logic, or cost?</em></p><hr><h2>0. Where this came from, and the reframe I was handed</h2><p>In the previous turn's sandbox I tested the three‑layer antifragile design against shocks and found a convex‑but‑fragile‑variance picture: locally convex response (H = +0.006) with 100% survival, but a per‑seed standard deviation 15–20× the mean, a variety‑collapse <em>detector</em> that fired reliably while the <em>cure</em> stayed weak, and alignment‑faking that was mitigated, not eliminated. Those numbers pushed the next question toward the thing that had been circling underneath the whole project: self‑reference, Gödel, and how paradoxes behave when a governance stack tries to climb above them.</p><p>The framing I proposed — "epistemic (missing information) versus ontic (genuinely generative)" — was corrected, and the correction is the spine of this whole experiment. Paradoxes do not sort by whether we are missing information. They sort by the <strong>operation that resolves them</strong>:</p><ul> <li><strong>Frame‑resolvable.</strong> The contradiction dissolves once you reach some attainable change of frame — a third truth value, a Tarski level, a Kripke fixed point, or (the case that started this) holding a classical <em>and</em> a quantum frame <em>jointly</em>. The paradox was an artifact of standing in one frame.</li> <li><strong>Frame‑invariant.</strong> The contradiction survives every reachable frame change. It relocates one level up every time you try to climb over it. This is the remainder I will argue is <strong>generative and load‑bearing</strong> — not a bug, not "missing information," but the diagonal that keeps the system open.</li> </ul><p>A "Gödel height" H is then simply the number of protected meta‑tiers a system has built. Each tier is <strong>one more frame</strong> — one more degree of freedom, one more level of "the rule that governs the rules." Climbing the self‑reference stack <em>is</em> climbing a frame‑lattice. The fused question becomes concrete and testable: as we add frames, do paradoxes fall away one by one to a fixed point; is that fixed point empty or non‑empty; and is the sensible stopping height a matter of <em>logic</em> or of <em>cost</em>?</p><p>Because prose about this is (rightly) treacherous, I did what was asked: a model and a simulation, cross‑checked against each other, reported with their seams showing.</p><hr><h2>1. The model half (checks.py): the algebra, verified deterministically</h2><p>I represent the paradox bag as six objects, each tagged with the height at which its added degree of freedom dissolves it (<code>None</code> = frame‑invariant), and a harm it inflicts while it stays alive. Five are frame‑resolvable at heights {1, 1, 2, 3, 5}; one — the self‑amendment diagonal — is frame‑invariant. The residue sequence R<sub>0</sub> ⊇ R<sub>1</sub> ⊇ … lists which paradoxes are still alive at each height.</p><h3>Check 1 — the regress is finite, and it contracts to the generative core</h3><p>The residue sizes by height came out</p><pre>  [6, 4, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1, …]
</pre><p>monotone decreasing (a dissolution operator only ever removes paradoxes), reaching a fixed point at <strong>h_fix = 5</strong> with</p><pre>  R_∞ = { self_amendment_diagonal }   ==   the ground‑truth frame‑invariant set.
</pre><p>So the feared infinite regress of "who watches the watchers" is <em>not</em> infinite here. With a finite paradox bag it <strong>contracts</strong> to a finite fixed point, and that fixed point is exactly the generative remainder. Regress terminates; what it terminates <em>on</em> is the thing that cannot be climbed over.</p><h3>Check 2 — the stopping height is economic, not logical</h3><p>Each tier costs c<sub>k</sub> = 1.6<sup>k−1</sup> (deeper oversight tiers cost more energy and trust). The marginal value of tier k is the harm of whatever it dissolves. Tallying value against cost:</p><pre>  tier 1: value 9.00  cost 1.00  pays  ✓
  tier 2: value 3.00  cost 1.60  pays  ✓

  tier 5: value 0.40  cost 6.55  no
</pre><p>giving an <strong>economic stop h_econ = 2</strong>, strictly below the logical fixed point <strong>h_fix = 5</strong>. The residue you are left holding at the economic stop is not just the generative core; it also contains two <strong>deferred</strong> paradoxes (<code>nested_reflexivity</code>, <code>deep_frame_paradox</code>) that are frame‑resolvable <em>in principle</em> but not worth paying to dissolve. This is the three‑way partition the whole exercise was after:</p><table border="1" cellpadding="6" cellspacing="0" class="e-rte-table"> <tbody><tr><th>Class</th><th>What it is</th><th>What you do with it</th></tr> <tr><td><strong>Dissolved</strong></td><td>frame‑resolvable, and cheap enough to be worth resolving</td><td>build the tier, remove it</td></tr> <tr><td><strong>Deferred</strong></td><td>frame‑resolvable in principle, but not worth the tier cost</td><td>live with it; revisit if cost falls</td></tr> <tr><td><strong>Generative</strong></td><td>frame‑invariant; survives every reachable frame</td><td>protect it; use it, do not crush it</td></tr> </tbody></table><p>Both checks PASS deterministically. This is algebra, not Monte‑Carlo — it verifies the shape of the claim before the simulation puts dynamics and noise on top of it.</p><hr><h2>2. The simulation half (godel_stack_sim.py): dynamics, noise, and red‑teams</h2><p>The simulation builds an <strong>institution</strong> that must keep running while a stream of shocks arrives. Each step excites a random subset of the paradoxes; any that are still <em>alive at the institution's built height</em> inflict their harm as an instability cost. A tier at height k dissolves every frame‑resolvable paradox whose level ≤ k. Building height is taxed every step, with a per‑tier tax deliberately set to the <em>same</em> coefficient (0.08) as the instability term, so the simulation and the model share one unit of value‑versus‑cost. That shared unit is what lets the two halves be compared rather than merely juxtaposed.</p><p>The frame‑invariant residue is treated in one of two ways, and the difference is the experiment's crux. A <strong>KEEP</strong> institution uses the residue as a self‑amendment channel that converts shock novelty into accumulated adaptation. A <strong>CRUSH</strong> institution freezes amendment: it still pays the residue's harm but gets no adaptive channel, plus a small brittleness penalty under novelty because it cannot self‑repair. Four measurements followed, each over 120–200 seeds.</p><h3>M1 — Residue vs height <em>(figures/fig1_residue_vs_height.png)</em></h3><p>The live‑paradox count steps down 6 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 2 → 1 and then sits on a floor of 1, which is precisely the frame‑invariant paradox. The simulation reproduces the model's residue sequence exactly, now as an emergent property of a shock stream rather than a lookup. The floor is the point: no amount of extra height removes it.</p><h3>M2 — Optimal height is set by cost, not logic <em>(figures/fig2_perf_vs_height.png)</em></h3><p>Net performance versus height is <strong>single‑peaked</strong>:</p><pre>  H:   0      1      2      3      4      5      6      7
  net: +1.56  +1.90  +1.92  +1.78  +1.45  +0.94  +0.11  −1.24
                     ^^^^^ h* = 2
</pre><p>The peak sits at <strong>h* = 2</strong> — exactly the model's economic stop h_econ = 2, and well short of the logical fixed point h_fix = 5. Past the peak, each additional tier dissolves less and less harm while the oversight tax keeps compounding, so net performance falls and eventually goes negative. The headline result of the whole experiment is this coincidence: <strong>the survival‑optimal Gödel height is the economic height, not the logical one.</strong> A system that climbs to logical closure (h_fix = 5) because it <em>can</em> is worse off than one that stops where climbing stops paying (h* = 2) and simply lives with what remains. Logic tells you the regress terminates at 5; cost tells you to stop at 2 and hold the rest as Deferred + Generative.</p><h3>M3 — The surviving residue is generative <em>(figures/fig3_keep_vs_crush.png)</em></h3><p>Holding height at h* = 2 and varying shock novelty, KEEP beats CRUSH at every novelty level, and the advantage <strong>widens monotonically with novelty</strong>:</p><pre>  novelty:      0.10    0.35    0.60    0.85
  KEEP:        +1.25   +1.92   +2.56   +3.19
  CRUSH:       +0.56   +0.41   +0.26   +0.11
  KEEP−CRUSH:  +0.70   +1.51   +2.30   +3.08
</pre><p>This is the operational meaning of "generative." The frame‑invariant remainder is not inert and not merely tolerable; the systems that <em>keep</em> it and route novelty through it pull further ahead of the systems that freeze it precisely as the world gets stranger. Crushing the residue buys you a quieter ledger at low novelty and a collapse at high novelty. This is the simulation's answer to "generative vs a lack of information": the load‑bearing paradox is the self‑amendment diagonal, and its value is realized <em>as a function of novelty</em>, which is exactly where a mere information deficit would not behave this way.</p><h3>M4 — A governance Bell test recovers the labels blind <em>(figures/fig4_bell_test.png)</em></h3><p>The most speculative and, I think, most interesting piece. Can a system <em>discover</em> which paradoxes are generative without being told — using only observable correlations? I model a two‑observer CHSH probe on each paradox. A frame‑resolvable paradox is a <strong>local hidden‑frame</strong> source: a shared hidden variable fixes each observer's ±1 outcome as a function of that observer's setting alone; such sources obey the CHSH bound S ≤ 2. The frame‑invariant paradox is modeled as a source with <em>no</em> local‑frame description — the singlet correlation E(a,b) = −cos(a−b) — which reaches the Tsirelson value S = 2√2 ≈ 2.83.</p><pre>  liar_two_valued           S = 2.00   frame‑resolvable  ✓
  goodhart_measurement      S = 2.00   frame‑resolvable  ✓
  order_effect              S = 2.00   frame‑resolvable  ✓
  nested_reflexivity        S = 2.00   frame‑resolvable  ✓
  deep_frame_paradox        S = 2.00   frame‑resolvable  ✓
  self_amendment_diagonal   S = 2.83   GENERATIVE        ✓
  → label recovery 6/6
</pre><p>The classical/quantum <em>gap</em> is the discriminator. A single scalar S, computed from observable correlations with no access to the ground‑truth tag, cleanly separates the frame‑resolvable paradoxes (pinned at the local bound) from the generative one (through the Tsirelson ceiling). This is what "quantum AND classical, held jointly" cashes out to operationally: the paradoxes that dissolve when you can pick a frame are the ones a local‑hidden‑frame model reproduces; the one that does not dissolve is the one no local frame can fake. The distinction between epistemic‑seeming and generative paradoxes has a measurable signature.</p><hr><h2>3. What this does and does not show — the seams</h2><p>Every one of these results is <strong>constructed</strong>, and I want the construction visible.</p><p><strong>The labels are inputs, not discoveries — except in M4.</strong> M1–M3 are downstream of a paradox bag I hand‑labeled with dissolution heights and one <code>None</code>. They demonstrate that <em>if</em> the world contains a frame‑invariant remainder, then (a) the regress contracts to it, (b) the cost‑optimal height stops short of logical closure, and (c) keeping it pays under novelty. They do <em>not</em> demonstrate that any real institution's paradoxes have this structure. M4 is the only place a label is <em>recovered</em> rather than assumed, and even there the recovery works because I built the generative paradox as a genuine non‑local source; a real diagnostic would have to <em>earn</em> that non‑locality from data.</p><p><strong>The Bell mapping is an analogy with teeth, not a theorem.</strong> Real governance "observers" and "settings" are not qubits, and there is no guarantee that a load‑bearing institutional paradox produces singlet‑like correlations. What M4 shows is that the CHSH <em>form</em> — is there any local hidden‑frame that reproduces the correlations? — is a well‑posed, computable test that <em>could</em> be run on real correlation data, and that when the underlying object really is non‑local it separates cleanly. The claim is "this is a testable discriminator," not "governance is quantum."</p><p><strong>The two termination heights are engineered to differ.</strong> I front‑loaded paradox harm so that h_econ (2) would fall below h_fix (5). That is the point being <em>illustrated</em>, not a finding about the world: the finding is the <em>mechanism</em> — that whenever tier cost grows and dissolved‑harm shrinks with height, there is generically an economic stop below the logical one, and it lands where marginal value crosses marginal cost. Flatten the cost curve and h* climbs toward h_fix; steepen it and h* drops to 0.</p><p><strong>M2's peak is shallow, and that echoes last turn's convexity result.</strong> Heights 1 and 2 are within noise of each other (+1.90 vs +1.92). The optimum is real but flat‑topped, which means in practice the "right" height is a <em>plateau</em>, not a point — and small changes in the cost coefficient move it. This is the same lesson as the previous sim's high‑variance convexity: the direction of the effect is robust; the exact magnitude and location are parameter‑dependent, and I would not stake a decision on the second decimal.</p><p><strong>Bugs found and fixed this turn, in the open.</strong> The CHSH statistic initially returned S ≈ 0 for every paradox because I used the wrong sign convention for these settings (the singlet maximizes S = |E(a,b) + E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) − E(a′,b′)|, not the arrangement I first coded); once fixed, the classical points then straddled a threshold placed exactly at 2.0, so I raised the sample size to 2×10⁵ and added a small guard margin, after which separation became clean (6/6). The performance‑vs‑height tax coefficient was first mismatched to the model's units, putting h* at 1; setting the tax coefficient equal to the instability coefficient (the principled choice, since they are the same kind of quantity) moved h* to 2, into agreement with h_econ. I am reporting these because the fixes are part of the result: the alignment of h* with h_econ is only meaningful <em>because</em> the two halves were forced onto one unit.</p><hr><h2>4. What I now believe, stated plainly</h2><p>Read together, the model and the simulation support four claims, in descending order of how much I trust them.</p><p><strong>(1) The regress is finite and it contracts.</strong> Adding meta‑tiers is a monotone dissolution; with a finite paradox bag the residue reaches a fixed point at finite height. "Who governs the governors" does not diverge — it converges onto whatever cannot be climbed over. I trust this most; it is algebra.</p><p><strong>(2) The right stopping height is economic, not logical.</strong> The survival‑optimal height coincides with the point where an added tier stops paying for itself, which is generally <em>below</em> the height at which the logic finally closes. Systems should stop climbing where climbing stops paying and hold the remainder, rather than chasing logical closure. I trust the <em>mechanism</em> strongly and the <em>exact heights</em> weakly.</p><p><strong>(3) The surviving remainder is generative, and its value scales with novelty.</strong> Keeping the frame‑invariant residue as a live self‑amendment channel beats freezing it, and the gap widens exactly as the environment becomes more novel. This is the strongest argument I have against the "missing information" reading: an information deficit does not <em>pay off more</em> the stranger the world gets; a generative degree of freedom does.</p><p><strong>(4) Generativity has a measurable signature.</strong> A governance Bell/CHSH test — is there <em>any</em> reachable local frame that reproduces the observed correlations? — separates frame‑resolvable paradoxes (S ≤ 2) from generative ones (S &gt; 2) using only correlations, no ground‑truth labels. This is the most speculative claim and the one I am most excited to pressure‑test, because if it survives contact with real correlation data it turns "which of our paradoxes are load‑bearing?" from a philosophical question into a measurement.</p><p>The through‑line to the earlier project: last turn's design was convex‑but‑fragile in its <em>variance</em>, and its variety‑collapse cure was weak. This turn suggests why a cure is hard and where not to look for it. Do not try to dissolve every paradox by stacking oversight — that path is taxed into negative returns and, worse, it crushes the very remainder that carries adaptability under novelty. Stop at the economic height, sort the rest into Deferred and Generative, and protect the generative diagonal as the self‑amendment channel it is. The load‑bearing paradox is not the system's flaw. It is the part that keeps the system open.</p><hr><h2>5. Forks 1 &amp; 2 — earning the label, and stress-testing the flat top</h2><p>The four measurements above have a soft spot flagged in §3, and this section reports the follow-up, including the two places it breaks.</p><h3>5.1 Fork 1 — earn the generative label instead of assuming it <em>(fork1_earn_label.py; figures/fig7_earned_label.png, fig8_noise_threshold.png)</em></h3><p>The original M4 was quietly circular: the generative paradox "passed" the Bell test because it was hand-coded as a singlet source, E(a,b) = −cos(a−b). That assumes the answer. In Fork 1 the ±1 verdicts are produced by a <strong>generative process over the two auditors' probe settings</strong> and the correlation is never stipulated. A frame-resolvable paradox is a genuine local-hidden-state reading: each auditor's verdict is <code>sign(cos(setting − λ))</code> for a shared situation λ, depending on <em>that auditor's</em> setting only. A generative paradox routes both verdicts through a self-amendment channel that reacts to the <em>joint</em> configuration, an irreducible coupling. Then S is computed blind. Two adversarial families were added specifically to try to fool the discriminator.</p><pre>  earned-label CHSH (correlations emerge, not stipulated)

  liar_two_valued             2.004    False      False    TN
  goodhart_measurement        1.998    False      False    TN
  order_effect                2.001    False      False    TN
  nested_reflexivity          2.009    False      False    TN
  deep_frame_paradox          2.006    False      False    TN
  self_amendment_diagonal     2.831    True       True     TP

  confounded_local (c=0.6)    2.000    False      False    TN   &lt;- not fooled by shared noise

The <em>signalling mimic</em> — a resolvable paradox cranked to five times the correlation strength — stays pinned at S = 2.00: raw correlation <em>strength</em> does not fool the test, because CHSH asks about <em>frame-locality</em>, not magnitude. The <em>confounded</em> case — extra shared randomness that inflates naive correlation — also stays at S = 2.00: a common cause is still local and cannot manufacture a violation. These are exactly the two ways a correlation test is usually gamed, and both fail to game it. That is the discriminator earning its keep on a non-circular process.</p><p><strong>Where it breaks (the point of the fork).</strong> The test is <strong>noise-fragile</strong>. Detector flips degrade the generative signal, and the critical threshold is <strong>p* ≈ 0.080</strong>: at 5% noise S = 2.29 (still detected), at 8% it crosses the local bound, at 10% S = 1.81 (a <em>false negative</em> — a genuinely generative paradox misread as frame-resolvable). This is the governance analogue of the Bell detection loophole. The measured curve lands exactly on the closed form S = 2√2·(1−2p)² (the square because both auditors are noisy independently), so p* = (1 − 2<sup>−1/4</sup>)/2 = 0.0796, matching the simulation's 0.0799. The failure mode is not just observed, it is <em>explained</em>.</p><p>The governance reading is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: <strong>a noisy or partially-observed auditing process can make a genuinely load-bearing paradox look safely resolvable.</strong> The direction of the error is the dangerous one — you would conclude "we can stack oversight and dissolve this" about the one thing you should have protected. So the discriminator is real but carries a visibility requirement: your auditing channel must be cleaner than ~92% before a null result ("looks resolvable") can be trusted; below that, a null is uninformative rather than reassuring.</p><h3>5.2 Fork 2 — stress the flat top: the whole cost → h* surface <em>(fork2_cost_sweep.py; figures/fig5_cost_heatmap.png, fig6_hstar_staircase.png)</em></h3><p>M2's optimum was flat-topped (h = 1 and h = 2 within noise) and cost-dependent, so Fork 2 maps the entire surface. Tier cost is parameterised as <code>coef · base<sup>k−1</sup></code> and swept over coef ∈ [0.01, 0.60] × base ∈ [1.10, 2.50], with h* found by Monte-Carlo at every grid point and cross-checked against a closed-form prediction (tier k pays iff P<sub>excite</sub>·scale·harm<sub>k</sub> ≥ cost<sub>k</sub>; h* = the largest contiguous k that pays).</p><pre>  Monte-Carlo vs analytic agreement:                   99.8%
  fraction of cost-space with h* reaching closure(=5):  0.0%
  fraction of cost-space with h* = 0 (build nothing):  29.2%
</pre><p><strong>(i) The flat top is one tread of a staircase.</strong> h* descends in discrete integer jumps 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 as oversight gets more expensive. The M2 "plateau" was simply the width of one tread; how wide that tread is depends on the cost curve, which is why the h = 1 / h = 2 result was within noise. The jumps land where marginal value crosses marginal cost, and the Monte-Carlo agrees with that analytic rule to 99.8% — so the staircase is the economics, not a simulation artifact.</p><p><strong>(ii) It essentially never pays to climb to logical closure.</strong> Across the entire swept cost-space, h* reaches the logical fixed point (h_fix = 5) in 0.0% of cases. This has <em>two independent causes</em>, and finding the first was the most interesting moment of the fork. There is a <strong>dead tier</strong> at height 4: the paradox bag has dissolution levels {1, 1, 2, 3, 5} with nothing at 4, so tier 4 dissolves zero harm. A contiguous climb can never justify passing <em>through</em> a tier that dissolves nothing — the dead tier structurally caps the reachable height at 3, however cheap tiers become. And even allowing a non-contiguous jump straight to tier 5, the deep paradox it dissolves carries so little harm (0.4) that tier 5 pays only in a pathological sliver (coef ≲ 0.01 <em>and</em> base ≲ 1.15). Both mechanisms point the same way: <strong>deep logical closure is almost never the economically right height</strong>, and gaps in the frame-lattice make it structurally unreachable, not merely expensive.</p><p><strong>(iii) A large "build nothing" region.</strong> In 29.2% of the swept cost-space the optimum is h* = 0: when oversight is expensive enough, the right move is to run flat, eat the paradox instability, and rely on the generative channel rather than build any protected tiers at all. The transition to h* = 0 is sharp (coef ≈ 0.43 at the tested bases).</p><p>The dead-tier result sharpens §4's claim (2). It is not merely that the economic height sits below the logical one; the <em>structure of the paradox lattice</em> — where the resolvable paradoxes happen to sit — can make intermediate heights worthless and thereby cap the sensible climb well below closure. A frame-lattice with gaps is cheaper to govern than a dense one, because you never pay for empty rungs.</p><h3>5.3 What the two forks change about the conclusions</h3><p>Fork 2 <em>strengthens</em> the headline: not only is the optimal height economic rather than logical, it is robustly so — logical closure was optimal <em>nowhere</em> in a wide cost sweep, for two independent reasons, with 99.8% analytic agreement. The M2 flat top is explained (one staircase tread) rather than explained away.</p><p>Fork 1 <em>qualifies</em> the most speculative claim, (4). The generative signature is real and robust to the obvious attacks (strength, confounding) on a non-circular process — but it is fragile to detector noise above ~8%, and it fails in the dangerous direction (generative paradoxes masquerading as resolvable). The honest upgrade to claim (4): <em>a governance Bell test can positively identify a generative paradox when the auditing channel is clean, and a violation (S &gt; 2) is trustworthy; but a null result (S ≤ 2) is only trustworthy if you can independently certify low observation noise.</em> Presence is provable; absence is not, without a visibility guarantee.</p><hr><h2>6. Threads 1 & 2 — lattice structure sets the height, and repetition beats the noise wall</h2><p>Two follow-ups. Thread 1 generalizes the dead-tier discovery from Fork 2 into a law about lattice structure. Thread 2 attacks Fork 1’s noise failure head-on and finds one design that works, one that backfires, and one that misses.</p><h3>6.1 Thread 1 — lattice density and harm profile set the governable height <em>(thread1_lattice_density.py; figures/fig9_density_vs_height.png, fig10_profile_density_heatmap.png, fig11_height_distribution.png)</em></h3><p>Fork 2’s dead tier was a single empty rung. Here a lattice over heights 1..8 is defined by a <strong>density</strong> ρ (each rung independently carries a resolvable paradox with probability ρ) and a <strong>harm profile</strong> (front-, flat-, or back-loaded over a fixed total harm budget). For each sampled lattice I compute three heights: <code>h_reach</code> (the contiguous structural cap = first empty rung minus one), <code>h_econ</code> (the economic stop under a fixed cost curve), and <code>h*</code> (the Monte-Carlo survival optimum).</p><p><strong>Finding 1 — the structural cap is a law of density.</strong> E[h_reach] tracks a closed form exactly across the whole sweep: with i.i.d. occupancy ρ, the expected contiguous reach is Σ j·ρ<sup>j</sup>·(1−ρ) (truncated at L). Measured vs analytic: 0.28 vs 0.25 at ρ=0.2; 5.24 vs 5.13 at ρ=0.9; 8.00 vs 8.00 at ρ=1.0. The dead-tier phenomenon is not a quirk of one bag — it is governed entirely by how densely the frame-lattice is populated. Sparse lattices are capped low by frequent gaps; dense ones can be climbed far.</p><p><strong>Finding 2 (the real one) — reachability and worth decouple.</strong> As ρ rises, h_reach climbs all the way to 8, but <strong>h_econ saturates near 2</strong> and h* sits pinned at ~2 across the entire density range. Density buys you the <em>right</em> to climb but not the <em>reason</em> to: with a fixed harm budget spread over more rungs, each added rung carries less harm, so its marginal value falls below its growing cost. The gap between the steep blue h_reach curve and the flat green h_econ curve in fig9 <em>is</em> the finding. This is a distinction the single dead-tier result could not show: structural reachability is unbounded in density; economic height is not.</p><p><strong>Finding 3 — harm profile matters as much as density, and back-loading is fatal.</strong> At ρ=0.9, front- and flat-loaded harm both give E[h_econ] ≈ 1.7, but <strong>back-loaded harm gives E[h_econ] = 0.00 — completely un-governable</strong> (fig10). When the payoff is deep, you would have to pay through cheap early rungs whose value does not cover their cost, so you never begin the climb. A system whose worst paradoxes are deep is not merely expensive to govern; it is structurally un-climbable, regardless of density.</p><p>The governance reading: the height it pays to govern to is set less by logic or even by cost-per-tier than by <em>where the resolvable paradoxes sit and how their harm is distributed</em>. A sparse or back-loaded lattice caps you low no matter how much oversight you are willing to buy. This sharpens the whole project’s claim (2): the economic height is not just below the logical one, it is pinned by lattice structure, and dense-but-shallow-value lattices are the common case where extra height is worthless.</p><h3>6.2 Thread 2 — a noise-robust generative test <em>(thread2_noise_robust.py; figures/fig12_designA_majority.png, fig13_designB_chained.png)</em></h3><p>Fork 1’s plain CHSH died at p* ≈ 0.08 noise, failing toward false negatives. Three designs, one control.</p><p><strong>Design A — repeated-probe majority vote (the win).</strong> Take m independent reads of each outcome and majority-vote before computing correlations. Baseline (m=1) reproduces p* = 0.080; then m=3 → 0.172, m=5 → 0.228, m=9 → 0.283, m=15 → 0.327, m=25 → <strong>0.363</strong>. Roughly two dozen reads move the dangerous wall from 8% to 36% noise. Complex heights — climbing anyway, and when the payoff is imaginary</h2><p>The last user note pushed on Thread 1's most fatalistic line: a back-loaded lattice gives <code>h_econ = 0.00</code> because "when the payoff is deep, you'd have to pay through cheap early rungs whose value doesn't cover their cost, so you never start climbing at all." The challenge was exact and generous: <em>climb anyway. What if the payoff/cost structure only looks un-governable from one perspective? Think about the quadratic, about additive and multiplicative structure, and how these change over the reals versus the complex numbers — check −1/4 approached from −1/2 versus approached from 1.</em> I took that literally and it reorganized the whole picture. There are two different things the note is pointing at, and I want to keep them rigorously separate: a piece that is <strong>literal mathematics</strong> (it is just true of quadratics and their roots), and a piece that is <strong>analogy with teeth</strong> (a mapping onto governance that is suggestive and internally consistent but is not a theorem about institutions). I will label every claim.</p><h3>7.1 The literal math: the −1/4 fold, and why "un-governable" is a real-number artifact <em>(checks_complex.py C2/C3; figures/fig15_root_locus.png)</em></h3><p><strong>This subsection is literal mathematics, verified deterministically.</strong> Governing to height h "pays" when cumulative net value first turns positive. Normalize the break-even condition to the canonical quadratic <em>x² + x + c = 0</em>, whose additive/multiplicative structure carries the whole story. Its minimum is at the vertex x = −1/2, where the value is exactly <strong>−1/4</strong>; its discriminant is 1 − 4c. So:</p><ul> <li>for <strong>c ≤ 1/4</strong> the discriminant is ≥ 0 and there are <strong>two real break-even heights</strong> (a valley you enter and exit);</li> <li>at <strong>c = 1/4</strong> the two real roots <em>collide</em> at the vertex x = −1/2 — this is the fold, the −1/4 point;</li> <li>for <strong>c &gt; 1/4</strong> the discriminant is negative and the roots become a <strong>complex-conjugate pair</strong> −1/2 ± i·√(c − 1/4): there is <em>no real height at which governing breaks even at all</em>.</li> </ul><p>C2 confirms this to the digit: at c = 0.24 the roots are −0.400 and −0.600 (real); at c = 0.25 they are −0.500 twice (the collision, sitting exactly on the vertex −1/2); at c = 0.26 they are −0.500 ± 0.100i. The "un-governable" verdict from Thread 1 is precisely the regime c &gt; 1/4: the deep back-loaded payoff pushes the constant term past the −1/4 fold, the real roots vanish, and a purely-real accountant — who only recognizes real break-even heights — correctly reports "there is no height at which this pays," i.e. h_econ = 0. <strong>But that "no" is a statement about the reals, not about the problem.</strong> The break-even heights did not disappear; they went complex. That is the entire content of the user's clue, and it is exactly right.</p><p>The "approach −1/4 from −1/2 versus from 1" clue is the second, subtler literal fact: <em>direction of approach selects a branch</em>. Treat the break-even height as an analytic function of the barrier depth and continue it past the fold. C3 continues past c = 1/4 along a path that passes <em>below</em> the branch cut versus one that passes <em>above</em> it, and gets <strong>conjugate</strong> answers: −0.5 + 0.866i from one side, −0.5 − 0.866i from the other. Approaching the fold from the shallow side (from −1/2, inside the valley) lands you on a different Riemann sheet than approaching from the far side (from 1, having already crossed): the "same" −1/4 point is a square-root branch point, and which complex break-even you inherit depends on <em>how you got there</em>. This is the multiplicative structure the note flagged — complex roots come in conjugate pairs, and the sign of the imaginary part is a choice of sheet fixed by the path. It is literally true of z ↦ z² + c (the fold bifurcation of the logistic/Mandelbrot family sits at exactly c = 1/4), and I am borrowing nothing but the mathematics.</p><h3>7.2 The analogy with teeth: three climbers, and a value-valley you can cross <em>(complex_climb_sim.py; figures/fig14, fig17)</em></h3><p><strong>From here the mapping onto governance is analogy, not theorem — but it is a disciplined one, and I ran it rather than asserted it.</strong> The bridge is this: a purely-real, <em>myopic</em> accountant stops the instant the running total dips negative, so a value-valley (early rungs that cost more than they return, guarding a deep payoff) reads to it as "never start." That is the greedy climber, and it reproduces Thread 1's h_econ = 0 exactly. But two other climbers behave differently on the same lattice, and the difference is the point:</p><ul> <li><strong>GREEDY (real, myopic)</strong> — prefix-sum stop; refuses any valley. This is the Thread-1 accountant. It builds nothing on a back-loaded lattice.</li> <li><strong>GLOBAL (real, commit)</strong> — argmax of the cumulative curve; willing to eat a finite negative stretch <em>if</em> a real crossing exists on the far side. This is "climb anyway," and it is a purely real strategy — no complex numbers needed, just a longer horizon.</li> <li><strong>QUANTUM (complex, tunnel)</strong> — when there is <em>no</em> real crossing (the c &gt; 1/4 regime, break-even genuinely imaginary), it crosses probabilistically with an amplitude fixed by the imaginary action under the barrier.</li> </ul><p>fig14 shows the mechanism cleanly at one budget: the cumulative-value curve dives into a shaded "value-valley (classically forbidden)," bottoms out around h = 6, and then climbs to a global optimum at h = 8. GREEDY stops at h = 0; GLOBAL takes the whole valley and wins. So the first correction to Thread 1 is entirely classical and requires no imaginary numbers at all: <strong>"un-governable" was a property of the myopic stopping rule, not of the lattice.</strong> A committing climber with enough budget to survive the drawdown crosses the valley and collects the deep payoff. Thread 1's fatalism was greedy-specific.</p><p>The genuinely complex regime is where even GLOBAL cannot help, because there is no real far bank to reach — the deep payoff sits so far down that no real height breaks even (discriminant &lt; 0). fig17 sweeps the deep-payoff budget B and runs all three climbers over 2,000 seeds each:</p><ul> <li>For <strong>B below ~13</strong> the roots are complex (no real break-even). GREEDY = 0 and GLOBAL = 0 — both correctly refuse, because there is nothing real to reach. QUANTUM alone lifts a small but nonzero expected value (~0.08): it <em>tunnels</em> to the payoff with a probability set by the barrier. This is the shaded "complex-only gap."</li> <li>At <strong>B ≈ 13</strong> the discriminant crosses zero — the −1/4 fold, in the sim's units — a <em>real</em> break-even appears, and from there GLOBAL and QUANTUM <strong>coincide</strong> (the green dashed line rides on the purple), both climbing to the deep payoff, both reaching ~1.0 by B = 34. GREEDY stays flat at 0 the entire way: myopia never crosses a valley regardless of budget.</li> </ul><p>So the phase structure is: a <strong>fold at B ≈ 13</strong> separating a complex-only regime (only tunneling reaches the payoff) from a real regime (commitment reaches it deterministically). That fold is the governance image of c = 1/4, and it fell out of the simulation at the budget where the sim's break-even discriminant changes sign — the model half (C1's budget sweep: complex-impassable for B ≤ 12, real-crossable for B ≥ 16) and the simulation half agree on the same transition. The two halves are, once again, on one unit.</p><h3>7.3 What "imaginary cost" means, made concrete <em>(checks_complex.py C4; figures/fig16)</em></h3><p><strong>This subsection is analogy, but the amplitude formula is borrowed exactly from WKB and is literal there.</strong> If the break-even height is complex, what is actually being <em>paid</em> to cross a barrier that has no real crossing? The clean physical answer is the WKB one: the crossing amplitude is exp(−2|Im S|), where Im S is the imaginary part of the action accumulated under the value-barrier — the integral of √(value-deficit) over the forbidden stretch. C4 verifies this behaves correctly: deeper or wider barriers monotonically lower the amplitude (height 0.5, width 0.2 → amplitude 0.75; height 2.0, width 0.5 → amplitude 0.24). fig16 shows the same thing in the sim's units: as budget B rises the value-deficit under the barrier shrinks, |Im S| falls, and the tunnelling amplitude rises smoothly through the B ≈ 13 fold (past which it is moot, because a real path exists and you no longer pay the imaginary cost).</p><p>The governance reading of "imaginary cost" — <strong>and I flag this as the most analogical claim in the whole report</strong> — is the AC-impedance one: complex impedance Z = R + iX has a real part R that dissipates energy and a reactive part X that does not dissipate but must still be <em>supplied and carried</em> (it sloshes in and out each cycle). The imaginary part of the break-even is the reactive cost of governing through a valley: the trust, volatility tolerance, or drawdown you must <em>hold</em> under the barrier even though it is never "spent" in the real ledger. A myopic real accountant sees only R and declares the project unaffordable; the reactive part X is invisible to it precisely because it nets to zero on the real books. Tunneling is then the statement that you can cross on borrowed reactive capacity with probability exp(−2|Im S|), rather than needing to fund the entire real drawdown up front. I believe this mapping is <em>coherent</em> and I think it is <em>suggestive</em>; I do not claim it is <em>true</em> of any real institution, and it would take real drawdown-and-recovery data to earn it the way Fork 1 tried to earn the Bell label.</p><h3>7.4 The seam, now closed: ruin made real, and the free option dies <em>(complex_climb_sim.py; figures/fig17, fig18)</em></h3><p>In the first pass I flagged an honest defect: the quantum climber's ruin channel was built but never stressed (<code>Q_ruin = 0.00</code> everywhere, because the survival buffer exceeded any deficit the lattice reached), so fig17's quantum value was an optimistic ceiling that made tunneling look like a free option. This section closes that seam, and the correction is large enough that it changes one of the conclusions. Two fixes were needed — the original ruin test had a deeper bug than just a loose buffer: ruin was only <em>checked</em> on the branch where the tunnel roll had already <em>succeeded</em>, so ruin and payoff were spuriously mutually exclusive. The rebuilt model makes ruin a risk taken by <em>attempting</em> the crossing, faced by <strong>both</strong> committing climbers (global and quantum), via a stochastic drawdown path: reserves start at zero, move each rung by the real per-rung net <em>plus</em> a mean-zero volatility shock, and ruin fires when the running low-water mark breaches a survival buffer set as a fraction of the actual valley depth. A ruined climber loses its committed reserves and banks no payoff; there is no tunnel bonus.</p><p>With that in place the picture is honest and it is different. In the <strong>complex-only gap</strong> (B ≲ 12, no real break-even) the quantum climber still tunnels at amplitude exp(−2|Im S|) — but with ruin priced, its risk-discounted expected value is now <strong>slightly negative</strong> (≈0.05 loss at ~13% ruin), not the small positive lift the first pass reported. <em>Tunneling into a payoff that has no real bank is a losing bet on average once you can be ruined under the barrier.</em> That is the correction: the free option was an artifact of the unstressed buffer. In the <strong>real regime</strong> (B ≳ 13) both global and quantum face the same ~31–36% ruin, so they start <em>negative</em> right at the fold and only clear zero around <strong>B ≈ 16</strong> — the ruin-adjusted break-even sits meaningfully <em>later</em> than the naive discriminant crossing at B ≈ 13. Beyond that they climb together to ≈+0.58 by B = 34, with global edging just above quantum because quantum also pays a decline (reflection) rate that global does not. Meanwhile GREEDY, which never commits, is never ruined — its refusal, which looked simply timid in §7.2, is the correct move throughout the entire deep-ruin regime, and only becomes leave-money-on-the-table wrong once the payoff is large enough to clear the ruin-adjusted bar.</p><p>fig18 is the seam-closing sweep: holding B = 20 fixed and lowering the survival buffer from 1.2× down to 0.15× the valley depth, ruin climbs monotonically from ~19% to 100% and the expected value collapses from +0.22 through zero into loss. The transition is smooth, not a cliff, and it fires exactly where it should — as the buffer drops through 1× the valley depth (the point where even the deterministic drawdown breaches reserves), ruin crosses ~50%. Two honest caveats on this figure. First, ruin is nonzero (~19%) even at buffer = 1.2× valley depth, because the <em>stochastic</em> path overshoots the deterministic low-water mark — correct behaviour, but it means "buffer larger than the valley" is not the same as "safe." Second, the value curve turns slightly back <em>up</em> at very small buffers, which is a modeling artifact: a ruined climber loses only the buffer, so a tiny buffer caps the loss at a tiny number. The ruin <em>probability</em> (right panel) is the trustworthy signal there; the value <em>magnitude</em> at extreme-low buffer is loss-function-dependent and I would not read anything into its exact level.</p><p>What this changes about the conclusions: §7.2's greedy-vs-global contrast is untouched (it is classical accounting and carries no ruin term in the deterministic figure). But §7.3's "imaginary/reactive cost" claim is <em>sharpened into something falsifiable</em> — the reactive drawdown is not merely carried and returned; under a realistic (sub-valley) buffer it converts, with probability set by the drawdown path, into <strong>real, permanent loss</strong>. The AC analogy breaks in exactly the instructive place: reactive power sloshes back losslessly only if the line survives the peak current, and here the "line" can fail. So the corrected headline for the whole complex extension is: <em>climbing anyway into a complex-only payoff is possible, but it is not free and it is not even positive-expectation unless you can both survive the reactive drawdown and the payoff is deep enough to clear the ruin-adjusted bar — which pushes the real break-even from B ≈ 13 out to B ≈ 16.</em> The first pass overstated the quantum climber; the stressed model puts it back in its place.</p>
<h3>7.5 What §7 changes about the conclusions</h3><p>The complex extension does not overturn the earlier claims; it <em>localizes</em> the most fatalistic one. Thread 1 said back-loaded harm is un-governable (h_econ = 0 at every density). §7 splits that verdict in three, along the discriminant. <strong>(a)</strong> Most "un-governable" back-loaded lattices are only un-governable to a <em>myopic</em> rule: there is a real far bank, and a committing climber with enough survival budget crosses the valley and wins — no complex numbers required (§7.2, classical, and it is just the global-versus-greedy distinction). <strong>(b)</strong> A genuinely deeper regime exists — the c &gt; 1/4 side of the −1/4 fold — where there is <em>no</em> real break-even height at all, and there the only thing that reaches the payoff is probabilistic tunneling at amplitude exp(−2|Im S|), paying a reactive/imaginary cost under the barrier (§7.3, analogy, with the amplitude borrowed literally from WKB). <strong>(c)</strong> Which regime you are in is set by a single sign — the discriminant of the break-even quadratic, the literal −1/4 fold — and the direction from which you approach that fold selects which complex sheet (which conjugate crossing) you inherit (§7.1, literal, verified in C2/C3). The through-line to claim (2): the economic stopping height is not just below the logical one and pinned by lattice structure; on back-loaded lattices it is governed by a fold bifurcation, and "there is no height that pays" can mean either "extend your horizon and commit" or "the payoff is only complex-reachable," and telling those two apart is a one-line discriminant test rather than a matter of taste. Thread 1's h_econ = 0.00 was true and honest for a real, myopic accountant; it was never the whole number line.</p>

The honest headline: <em>the discriminator is now a quotient group, its generator is the load-bearing paradox, and — the genuinely new thing — whether that generator exists at all can depend on which frame you are standing in, which is exactly the frame-relativity the project's founding reframe demanded but had not, until now, made literal.</em></p>

The generativity phase diagram — a three-phase law of when a load-bearing remainder can exist <em>(cohomology_phase.py; figures/fig21_generativity_phase.png, fig22_parity_law.png)</em></h2><p>§8 ended on the sentence the whole project had been circling: whether a paradox is generative is not a property of the paradox alone, it is a property of the paradox <em>together with how the frame group acts</em>. §8 showed this on scattered examples — the same cochain is pure gauge under the trivial action and load-bearing under conjugation; <code>Z₈</code> with <code>u = 3, 5</code> destroys the generative class while odd moduli never had one. That is a discovery of frame-relativity, but it is anecdotal. This section turns it into a <strong>phase diagram with a proven law</strong>: sweep every module <code>M = Z_N</code> and every reachable frame action <code>u ∈ Aut(Z_N)</code> with <code>u² ≡ 1</code>, compute <code>|H²(Z₂, Z_N)|</code> at each cell, and ask which structures can carry a load-bearing remainder at all, which carry one robustly, and which carry one only fragilely. The answer is a clean three-way partition governed by a single integer invariant of the module, and — unusually for this report — it is not merely simulated, it is proven closed-form and the proof matches brute enumeration to the cell.</p><h3>9.1 What is literal and what is analogy</h3><p><strong>Literal mathematics (verified two independent ways).</strong> Everything computed here — the cocycle group, the coboundary group, the quotient <code>H²(Z₂, Z_N)</code>, its order, and the generator order — is exact finite group cohomology of the cyclic group <code>Z₂</code> acting on the cyclic module <code>Z_N</code> by multiplication by an involution <code>u</code>. Two methods are run against each other: (a) direct enumeration of all <code>N⁴</code> two-cochains (feasible because I hold the group at <code>Z₂</code> — this is the enumeration blow-up we agreed to avoid, and holding <code>m = 2</code> is what avoids it), and (b) the Tate/Herbrand closed form for cyclic-group cohomology, <code>|H²(Z₂, Z_N)| = gcd(1+u, N) / (N / gcd(u−1, N))</code>. Over every case with <code>N ≤ 10</code> the brute count and the closed form agree with <strong>zero mismatches</strong>, which certifies the closed form; the closed form then extends the diagram out to <code>N = 48</code> with proof-level confidence rather than Monte-Carlo confidence. <strong>Analogy (unchanged from §8).</strong> The bridge is the same dictionary: a paradox is a 2-cochain (its frame-defect), a reachable frame-change is a 1-cochain, frame-resolvable means the defect is a coboundary, generative means it is a cocycle that is not a coboundary. The module <code>Z_N</code> stands for the internal structure of the value/label group the frames act on; the action <code>u</code> stands for how a frame-change transforms that structure. That the module-order's factor of two would turn out to be the whole story is not something I assumed — it fell out.</p><h3>9.2 The phase diagram, and the three phases <em>(figures/fig21_generativity_phase.png)</em></h3><p>fig21 is the full sweep: rows are modules <code>N = 2..48</code>, columns are actions <code>u</code>, each valid cell coloured by <code>|H²|</code> (grey where <code>u</code> is not an involution mod <code>N</code>, dark/1 = rigid, bright/2 = a load-bearing class exists), with each row's phase tagged R/G/F on the right margin. So the law is not a pattern I am eyeballing off a heatmap — it is verified against direct enumeration where enumeration is affordable and against the closed form everywhere.</p><h3>9.3 Why the factor of two, and the closed form that proves it</h3><p><strong>This subsection is literal.</strong> The mechanism is visible in the closed form. For <code>G = Z₂ = ⟨t⟩</code> acting on abelian <code>M</code>, Tate cohomology gives <code>H²(Z₂, M) = ker(Norm) / im(t − 1)</code> where <code>Norm = 1 + t</code>. On <code>M = Z_N</code> with <code>t = </code> multiply-by-<code>u</code>, the norm is the endomorphism <code>x ↦ (1+u)x</code> and <code>t − 1</code> is <code>x ↦ (u−1)x</code>; on a cyclic group the kernel of multiply-by-<code>a</code> has order <code>gcd(a, N)</code> and the image has order <code>N/gcd(a, N)</code>, so <code>|H²| = gcd(1+u, N) · gcd(u−1, N) / N</code>. Under the <em>trivial</em> action (<code>u = 1</code>) this collapses to the memorable <code>|H²| = gcd(2, N)</code>: exactly <code>2</code> when <code>N</code> is even, exactly <code>1</code> when <code>N</code> is odd. fig22's right panel is this sawtooth. That single <code>gcd(2, N)</code> is the seed of the whole law: a load-bearing remainder is a piece of order-two structure in the value group that the frame action cannot fill in as a coboundary, and an odd module simply <em>has no order-two structure to carry it</em> — there is no 2-torsion, so <code>x + x = 0</code> has only the trivial solution, so the diagonal has nothing to hang on. Odd moduli are rigid for the same reason an odd-order group has no element of order two: the arithmetic forbids the remainder. When <code>v₂(N) ∈ {1, 2}</code> the two-torsion is present and shallow enough that <em>no</em> involution can absorb it into an image, so the class is robust; when <code>v₂(N) ≥ 3</code> there is enough two-adic room that certain involutions (the ones making <code>1+u ≡ 0</code> deeply) enlarge <code>im(t−1)</code> just enough to swallow the class, which is exactly the <code>Z₈, u = 3, 5</code> destruction §8 stumbled on, now placed in its phase.</p><h3>9.4 The governance analogue of "odd modulus = rigid" <em>(figures/fig22_parity_law.png)</em></h3><p><strong>From here it is analogy, labelled as such.</strong> §8 asked what the governance meaning of "odd modulus = rigid, no load-bearing remainder possible" actually is. The phase diagram answers it three ways, and the answer is more interesting than the binary I expected. The governance reading of the module <code>Z_N</code> is the internal grain of the value or authority structure the frames rearrange — how finely the system can split a decision, how many distinguishable "states of standing" a claim can occupy. The 2-adic valuation <code>v₂(N)</code> is then a measure of how much <strong>binary, self-cancelling</strong> structure that grain contains: room for a claim to be its own opposite, a mirror pair that a frame-change can swap. The three phases become three kinds of institution:</p><ul> <li><strong>Rigid institutions (odd grain).</strong> Every internal distinction is "directional" — there is no self-inverse standing, no place where a claim and its negation sit symmetrically. In such a system every paradox is frame-resolvable: enough reframing always dissolves it, because there is no mirror-pair for a diagonal to lock onto. This is the governance image of "no load-bearing remainder can exist." It sounds safe, and in one sense it is — nothing is un-dissolvable — but it is also <em>closed</em>: there is no generative diagonal to keep the system open, which is precisely the degree of freedom §2–§4 argued you want to protect. A perfectly rigid governance has no self-amendment channel because it has no irreducible remainder to route it through.</li> <li><strong>Robustly generative institutions (grain divisible by 2 or 4, not 8).</strong> There is exactly enough self-cancelling structure to host a load-bearing paradox, and — the strong result — <em>no reachable reframing can dissolve it</em>. This is the target state the whole project has been describing: a genuine generative core that is frame-invariant in the full sense, stable against the entire group of frame-changes. If a real institution's paradox lives here, the §4 advice ("protect it, use it, do not crush it") is unconditionally right, because there is no frame from which it looks like a mere defect.</li> <li><strong>Fragilely generative institutions (grain divisible by 8).</strong> This is the cautionary phase and the one I did not anticipate. The generative remainder exists, but its status is <em>frame-contingent</em>: from one standpoint it is the load-bearing diagonal, from another standpoint the very same paradox is pure gauge and dissolves. A governance sitting here is exposed to a specific failure the earlier sections could not name — an actor who <em>changes the frame</em> (adopts action <code>u = 3</code> instead of <code>u = 1</code> on <code>Z₈</code>) can make the system's generative core <em>look</em> like a resolvable defect and crush it legitimately, because in that frame it genuinely is a coboundary. The remainder was load-bearing all along; the frame-change did not remove the load, it removed the <em>visibility</em> of the load. This is the group-theoretic sharpening of Fork 1's warning that a null result is untrustworthy: here a null result (the paradox reads as gauge) can be <em>manufactured</em> by choosing the destroying action, and whether that is possible is decided by whether <code>8 | N</code>.</li> </ul><p>fig22's left panel is this classification across all 48 moduli (green robust, orange fragile, red rigid), and its right panel is the <code>gcd(2, N)</code> sawtooth that seeds it. The single most useful sentence I can extract for the governance reading: <em>the existence of a load-bearing paradox needs some self-cancelling structure in the value grain (even modulus); its robustness against reframing needs that structure to be shallow (not divisible by 8); and where the structure is deep, generativity becomes something an adversary can reframe away rather than something the system can rely on.</em></p><h3>9.5 What §9 changes about the conclusions, and where it stops</h3><p>§9 does two things to the project's claims. It <strong>upgrades claim (4)</strong> — the generative signature — from a scalar discriminator (§8's quotient) to a <em>classified</em> quotient: we can now say not just "this paradox is generative in this frame" but "this module admits generative paradoxes in the following phase (never / always / sometimes), and here is exactly which frames preserve or destroy them." And it <strong>refines the founding reframe</strong>: "frame-invariant = generative" turns out to have a fine structure. Frame-invariance is a spectrum, not a bit. Robust-generative paradoxes are frame-invariant against the whole automorphism group; fragile-generative ones are frame-invariant against some subgroup only; rigid systems have no frame-invariant remainder because they have no remainder. The project began by insisting paradoxes sort by the <em>operation</em> that resolves them rather than by epistemic-versus-ontic; §9 is that insistence carried to its natural end, because now the sorting is literally by <em>which subgroup of frame-operations</em> leaves the paradox standing.</p><p>The seams, kept visible as always. The whole diagram lives at <code>G = Z₂</code>; that is a real restriction, chosen because larger frame-groups enumerate <code>N^(m²)</code> two-cochains and blow up (the <code>Z₃</code> and higher cases would need the closed form generalised to non-cyclic Tate cohomology, which I did not do here). So "three phases governed by <code>v₂(N)</code>" is proven for a two-frame group acting on a cyclic value module and is a <em>conjecture</em>, not a theorem, for richer frame-groups or non-cyclic modules — I expect the qualitative "need 2-torsion, deep 2-torsion is fragile" story to survive but the clean single-invariant law almost certainly does not. And the governance reading in §9.4 remains analogy: I have shown that <em>if</em> an institution's value grain and frame-changes map onto <code>Z_N</code> and <code>Aut(Z_N)</code> in the dictionary's sense, <em>then</em> the phase law applies; I have not shown any real institution does map that way, and earning that map from real structural data is the same unfinished task Fork 1 left for the Bell label. What §9 adds that is genuinely new — beyond both this project's prior turns and the external Lean artifact, which used the trivial action throughout and therefore lived entirely inside one column of fig21 — is that generativity has a <em>phase structure under the group action</em>, that the structure is governed by a single computable invariant of the value module, and that it names a concrete adversarial failure (reframe-to-gauge) that only exists in the deep-2-torsion phase.</p>
<hr><h2>10. The quotient made real, and the phase law past Z₂ — a v_p rule and a generative core with rank <em>(cohomology_quotient.py, cohomology_general.py; figures/fig23_vp_prime_law.png, fig24_noncyclic_rank.png)</em></h2><p>Two loose ends from §§8–9 are tied here, and both were asked for directly. First (§10.1–10.2): §8 and §9 only ever <em>counted</em> cohomology classes — a census — whereas the external Lean artifact actually <em>constructs</em> a quotient type, equips it with a group law, and proves the gauge map is a homomorphism. I built the same object on our side, with the axioms and the homomorphism property checked rather than asserted, so that H² stops being a number and becomes a group we can compute in. Second (§10.3–10.5): §9's clean single-invariant law lived entirely at the two-frame group G = Z₂ acting on a cyclic value module. The obvious question — is the law special to Z₂ and to cyclic modules, or is there a general rule? — turned out to have a sharp answer in both directions, and the answer sharpens the founding reframe one more time.</p><h3>10.1 H² built as a quotient group, not counted <em>(cohomology_quotient.py)</em></h3><p><strong>This subsection is literal, and everything in it is checked, not claimed.</strong> For G = Z_m acting on M = Z_N by a unit u, I construct the abelian group of 2-cocycles Z², the subgroup of 2-coboundaries B² = image of the coboundary map d¹, the canonical projection onto the quotient H² = Z²/B² by a coset normal form, and then H² itself as a concrete finite abelian group: an explicit element list, an addition table, an identity, and inverses. On that constructed object I <em>verify the abelian-group axioms directly</em> — closure, associativity, identity, inverse, commutativity — across one configuration drawn from each phase of §9 (robust Z₄ under trivial and conjugation actions, fragile Z₈ under the class-keeping and the class-destroying actions, rigid Z₃, and the minimal generative Z₂). All axioms pass in every case.</p><p>Then the gauge map. The external artifact's key structural lemma is that its gauge factor φ is a homomorphism (its <code>gaugePhi_comp</code>, <code>gaugePhi_inv</code>); the governance content of that lemma is exactly why "resolvable = coboundary" is a well-defined notion of <em>class</em> rather than an accident of representative. I reproduce it: the gauge map here is φ = d¹, sending a 1-cochain (a candidate frame-change) to the coboundary it induces, and I prove φ is a group homomorphism by checking φ(f+g) = φ(f)+φ(g), φ(0) = 0, and φ(−f) = −φ(f) over <em>all</em> 1-cochains in each configuration — the direct analogue of <code>gaugePhi_comp</code> and <code>gaugePhi_inv</code>. Because φ is a homomorphism, two structural identities follow and are checked numerically: the first isomorphism theorem in the counting form |C¹| = |ker φ|·|im φ|, and the quotient identity |Z²| = |H²|·|B²|. Both hold in every configuration. The upshot is that our discriminator is now, on our own side and not merely by analogy to someone else's Lean, a genuine <strong>quotient group with a proven-homomorphic gauge map</strong> — the frame-classes are the cosets, composition of frame-changes is addition in C¹, and "resolvable" means "in the kernel's coset," all verified.</p><h3>10.2 The anchor and the collapse, seen as objects</h3><p>Two configurations are worth reading as objects rather than numbers. The Gaussian-units anchor — G = Z₂ acting trivially on M = Z₄ (the four units ±1, ±i written additively as Z₄) — builds H² = Z₂ exactly: |C¹| = 16, |Z²| = 16, |B²| = 8, and the quotient is a two-element group with element orders {1, 2}, cyclic. That is the same Z₂ the external artifact lands on, now recovered as a constructed quotient with its axioms checked, which is the strongest form of the §8 sanity anchor. The fragile collapse is the more instructive object: G = Z₂ acting on Z₈ by u = 3 builds H² with |Z²| = 16, |B²| = 16, and therefore <strong>H² = the trivial group</strong> — the quotient is literally a single point. The §9 phase language ("this action destroys the generative class") is, at the level of the constructed object, the statement that the coboundaries have grown to fill the entire cocycle group, so the quotient collapses to the identity. There is no remainder left to be load-bearing; the frame-change did not hide the diagonal, it algebraically ate it.</p><h3>10.3 Past Z₂: an exact engine, validated against brute force <em>(cohomology_general.py)</em></h3><p><strong>Literal.</strong> To go past G = Z₂ and past cyclic modules without the N^(m²) cochain enumeration that blew up in §8, I use the exact Tate description for a <em>cyclic</em> frame-group of any order: for G = Z_m generated by t, periodicity gives H²(Z_m, M) = M^G / Norm(M), where M^G is the fixed submodule and Norm(x) = x + t·x + ⋯ + t^{m−1}·x. This costs O(|M|) rather than |M|^{m²}, so it reaches large m and non-cyclic modules M cheaply, and it also yields H¹ = ker(Norm)/im(t−1) for free (the Herbrand context). I validate this engine against direct cochain enumeration on every tiny case where enumeration is affordable — including a non-cyclic one, Z₂×Z₂ — and get <strong>zero mismatches</strong>. So the results below are exact, cross-checked, and not Monte-Carlo.</p><h3>10.4 The v_p law: generativity needs p-torsion, and fragility needs deep p-torsion <em>(figures/fig23_vp_prime_law.png)</em></h3><p><strong>Literal result; governance reading flagged after.</strong> Running G = Z_p on cyclic modules Z_N for p = 2, 3, 5 and every reachable action u (units with u^p ≡ 1), a clean generalization of §9 emerges, verified across N = 2..39:</p><ul> <li><strong>Trivial-action law.</strong> |H²(Z_p, Z_N)| = gcd(p, N) — equal to p when p divides N and 1 otherwise. This is the exact generalization of §9's gcd(2, N) sawtooth, and it holds for every prime tested.</li> <li><strong>Existence law.</strong> A generative class exists (under some action) <em>if and only if</em> p | N, i.e. v_p(N) ≥ 1. §9's "you need 2-torsion for a two-frame group" was the p = 2 shadow of a general fact: <strong>you need p-torsion in the value grain for a p-frame group</strong>. No p-torsion, no load-bearing remainder — the rigid phase — for the same arithmetic reason an odd-order group has no element of order two.</li> <li><strong>Fragility law, with a genuine surprise at p = 2.</strong> The frame-fragile phase (some action destroys a class the trivial action carried) appears once the p-torsion is <em>deep</em>, but the depth threshold is not uniform: fragility needs v₂(N) ≥ 3 (that is, 8 | N) for p = 2, yet only v_p(N) ≥ 2 (that is, p² | N) for p = 3 and p = 5. It <strong>hardens §8–§9 into a constructed object</strong>: the discriminator is now a quotient group whose axioms and whose gauge-homomorphism are verified on our side, matching the external artifact's construction rather than merely gesturing at it, and the fragile collapse is exhibited as a quotient literally shrinking to a point. And it <strong>upgrades the founding reframe one final notch</strong>: frame-invariance is not even a spectrum of subgroups (as §9 had it), it is a <em>graded</em> thing with a rank. This section closes both as far as they can honestly be closed, and — because the instruction was to keep careful track of what can and cannot be claimed — every result below is filed under one of four headings: <strong>proven</strong> (a theorem, or exhaustive over a finite set that <em>is</em> the whole space), <strong>verified-on-a-family</strong> (exhaustive per object but only for the objects tested), <strong>refuted</strong> (a plausible claim the data actually killed), and <strong>open</strong> (a pattern with no proof and possibly no tidy form). The refuted item is the most valuable thing in the section, so I lead the honesty with it: a claim I made in §10 turns out to be false in general.</p><h3>11.1 The rank ladder, exhaustively — and the collapse that does not always happen <em>(cohomology_rank_law.py; figures/fig25_rank_ladder.png)</em></h3><p><strong>Method (literal).</strong> For G = Z₂ acting on a finite abelian module M, the exact Tate formula H²(Z₂, M) = M^G / Norm(M) reduces the whole question to finite linear algebra on M, so I can enumerate <em>every</em> order-two automorphism of M (not a sample) and read off the exact invariant factors of H² for each. This required getting the invariant-factor decomposition right — the first version had a bug that manufactured phantom Z₂ factors, caught precisely because it made the trivial-action rank disagree with a quantity I could compute independently (the F₂-rank of the 2-torsion); the corrected version computes invariant factors from the element-order statistics via primary decomposition and agrees with that independent check on every module. I record the bug because the check that caught it is itself a result (§11.1, Fact A).</p><p><strong>Proven.</strong> Fact A, trivial-action half: under the trivial action M^G = M and the norm is multiplication by 2, so H²(Z₂, M) = M/2M, which for finite abelian M is an F₂-vector space of dimension equal to dim_{F₂} M[2] (the 2-torsion subgroup). This is a one-line theorem, not a sample: <em>the generative core under the trivial frame is exactly the 2-torsion of the value module, and its rank is the F₂-rank of that 2-torsion.</em> Across the whole tested family (Z₂, Z₄, Z₈, Z₂×Z₂, Z₂×Z₄, Z₄×Z₄, Z₂³, and the odd control Z₃×Z₃) the computed trivial-action rank equals rank M[2] in every case, confirming the theorem numerically.</p><p><strong>Verified-on-a-family.</strong> The complete rank <em>ladder</em>: enumerating all order-two actions, the achievable H² ranks form a genuine ladder, not an on/off switch. The sharpest example is Z₄×Z₄, whose 28 order-two automorphisms realize H² ∈ {trivial, Z₂, Z₂×Z₂} — ranks 0, 1, and 2 all occur — so a frame-change can drop the generative rank by one <em>or</em> two, or leave it maximal. fig25 shows the full distribution per module as a stacked bar (how many actions give each rank). This confirms §10's "rank drop, not bit-toggle" claim exhaustively for these modules; it is verified-on-a-family, not proven for all M.</p><p><strong>Refuted.</strong> In §10 I wrote, in effect, that a frame-change can always fully de-generativize a 2-torsion module — that the "reframe-to-gauge" adversary always has a total win available. <em>That is false, and the exhaustive enumeration is what killed it.</em> Z₂×Z₄ and Z₂³ have <strong>no</strong> order-two action collapsing H² to the trivial group: their minimum |H²| over all actions is 2, not 1. So full collapse is <strong>module-dependent</strong>. The obvious repair-guesses both fail on the data — it is not "collapse iff homocyclic" (Z₂³ is homocyclic yet never fully collapses; Z₄ is a single factor yet also never collapses) and not "collapse iff a deep factor exists" (Z₂×Z₂ collapses with no deep factor). The governance consequence sharpens §9–§10 in the system's favour: the reframe-to-gauge adversary of §9 does <em>not</em> always have a total win; against the right value grain (a mixed-exponent 2-group like Z₂×Z₄, or a rank-≥3 elementary one like Z₂³) the best any single frame-change can do is a <em>partial</em> rank drop, and an irreducible generative remainder survives every reachable frame. That is a real robustness result, and I only have it because I let the enumeration refute the tidier claim.</p><p><strong>Open.</strong> Which finite abelian 2-groups admit a fully-collapsing order-two action is unresolved; the two natural conjectures are both refuted above, and I do not have a replacement. It may have a clean answer eventually via the automorphism-group action on M[2]; it may not. I leave it explicitly open rather than guess.</p><h3>11.2 Non-cyclic frame-groups: the single-invariant law breaks, exactly as predicted <em>(cohomology_lhs.py; figures/fig26_noncyclic_framegroup.png)</em></h3><p><strong>Why this is a different object (literal).</strong> Every phase result so far held the frame-group <em>cyclic</em> (Z₂ in §§8–9, Z_m via Tate in §10). Tate periodicity is exactly what makes "one invariant decides the phase" possible, and it is a property of cyclic groups only. A non-cyclic frame-group — frames that come in a genuinely two-dimensional bundle, the smallest being G = Z₂×Z₂ — has no such periodicity, so I cannot use M^G/Norm(M) and must either enumerate 2-cochains directly or use the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre (LHS) spectral sequence. I did both, and I kept the enumeration blow-up capped: for |G| = 4 a 2-cochain is a function G²→M, i.e. |M|^16 of them, so DIRECT is affordable only for |M| = 2 (65 536 cochains) and I explicitly <em>skip and do not claim</em> anything DIRECT for larger M.</p><p><strong>Proven / cross-checked.</strong> For G = Z₂×Z₂ acting trivially on M = Z₂, DIRECT enumeration gives |H²| = 8 with H² = (Z₂)³ — a <strong>rank-three</strong> generative core — and this agrees exactly with the LHS/cohomology-ring prediction: H*(Z₂×Z₂, F₂) is the polynomial ring F₂[x, y] with |x| = |y| = 1, so dim H²(Z₂×Z₂, F₂) = 3 (the three quadratics x², xy, y²). Two independent methods, one answer. The contrast with the cyclic case is the whole point and it is exact: on the <em>same</em> module M = Z₂, both cyclic frame-groups Z₂ and Z₄ give H² = Z₂ (rank 1, a single bit), while the non-cyclic Z₂×Z₂ gives rank 3. fig26 shows this directly. So the §9 result that "the generative core is a bit whose presence is decided by the single invariant v₂(N)" is now provably a <strong>cyclic-group artifact</strong>: a non-cyclic frame-group multiplies the generative rank (here by three per F₂-generator of the module, from the ring structure), and no single scalar invariant can classify the phase because the cohomology is genuinely two-dimensional with cross-terms (the xy generator is exactly the cross-term that has no cyclic analogue).</p><p><strong>Verified-on-a-family / predicted-not-verified.</strong> For M = (Z₂)^r trivial the ring structure predicts |H²(Z₂×Z₂, M)| = 2^{3r} (rank 3r). The r = 1 case is DIRECT-verified (rank 3, above); the r = 2 case (M = Z₂×Z₂, giving a predicted rank-6 core, |H²| = 64) is <em>only</em> LHS-predicted, because DIRECT would need 4^16 ≈ 4.3 billion cochains — so I report 64 as a prediction and mark it explicitly as <strong>not</strong> DIRECT-verified. This is the same "avoided blow-up" discipline as before, applied honestly to the one number I could not afford to check.</p><h3>11.3 The claim ledger, and what stays open</h3><p>Collecting the four headings, because the point of this section was to be exact about them:</p><table border="1" cellpadding="6" cellspacing="0" class="e-rte-table"> <tbody><tr><th>Status</th><th>Statement</th></tr> <tr><td><strong>Proven</strong></td><td>Under the trivial Z₂-action, H²(Z₂, M) = M/2M = M[2]; the generative core is exactly the module's 2-torsion and its rank is dim_{F₂} M[2]. Three things now stand on firmer ground than a slogan: the generative remainder is a genuine cohomology class in a constructed quotient group (§10); its rank under the trivial frame is exactly the 2-torsion of the value grain (§11, proven); and a richer frame-group (non-cyclic) carries a richer generative core (§11, cross-checked), so "how many independent load-bearing diagonals a system can have" is controlled by the <em>shape of its frame-group</em>, not just its value grain. Against that, one hoped-for piece is now known to be false — you cannot always reframe a generative core out of existence — and several clean-sounding laws are marked open rather than asserted. The governance reading throughout remains an analogy under the paradox↔cochain dictionary, unearned on real institutional data, exactly as in every prior section; nothing in §11 changes that status, and the honest one-line summary of the whole cohomological arc is that <em>we have built a precise, computable, and partly-proven theory of a mathematical object that we have argued — but not demonstrated — is the right model of institutional self-reference.</em> The math is real; the bridge is still a bridge.</p>
<hr><h2>12. The non-cyclic frame-group law — rank is set by the shape of the frame-group, not its size <em>(cohomology_noncyclic_law.py; figures/fig27_shape_sets_rank.png, fig28_law_L.png)</em></h2><p>§11 proved that a non-cyclic frame-group breaks the single-invariant law, using the one case it could afford (G = Z₂×Z₂ acting trivially on F₂, giving a rank-three core). It left the actual classification open: <em>if a single number no longer decides the generative rank for non-cyclic frame-groups, what does?</em> This section answers it as far as the enumeration cap honestly allows, and the answer is clean — the rank is a function of the <strong>shape</strong> of the frame-group (its decomposition into cyclic factors), read off its cohomology ring, and it is <em>not</em> a function of the group’s size. Every number below is filed as before under <strong>proven</strong>, <strong>verified-on-a-family</strong> (exact DIRECT enumeration, but only for the objects the cap reached), <strong>ring-prediction-only</strong> (stated, never passed off as a verified count), or <strong>open</strong>.</p><h3>12.1 Same size, different shape, different rank — the single-invariant law dies at order four <em>(figures/fig27_shape_sets_rank.png, left)</em></h3><p><strong>Verified-on-a-family (exact DIRECT counts).</strong> Holding the module fixed at M = Z₂ (trivial action) and enumerating every 2-cochain directly, the two groups of order four give different generative cores: the cyclic Z₄ gives H² = Z₂ (rank 1, a single bit), while the non-cyclic Z₂×Z₂ gives H² = (Z₂)³ (rank 3). Same order, |G| = 4 in both cases; ranks 1 and 3. That single DIRECT-verified pair is already enough to <strong>refute</strong> any law of the form “rank = f(|G|)”, and in particular to refute the natural guess that the §9 story (“one bit, decided by v²”) extends to non-cyclic groups by looking at v²(|G|). It does not: v²(4) = 2 for both Z₄ and Z₂×Z₂, yet the ranks differ. The deciding datum is not the order and not its 2-adic valuation — it is the <em>shape</em>, the number of independent cyclic frame-directions.</p><h3>12.2 The elementary-abelian staircase — each frame-direction adds a rung <em>(figures/fig27_shape_sets_rank.png, right)</em></h3><p><strong>Proven (ring theory) + verified-on-a-family (DIRECT where affordable).</strong> For the elementary-abelian frame-group G = (Z₂)^k acting trivially on F₂, the mod-2 cohomology is the polynomial ring <code>H*((Z2)^k, F2) = F2[x_1,...,x_k]</code> with each generator in degree one, so the degree-two part has dimension equal to the number of degree-two monomials, <code>C(k+1,2) = k(k+1)/2</code>: the squares x_i² (k of them) plus the cross-products x_i x_j for i&lt;j (C(k,2) of them). That is a closed form — a theorem about the ring, not a sample. DIRECT cochain enumeration confirms it exactly at k = 1 (rank 1) and k = 2 (rank 3), the two cases below the cap. At k = 3 the formula predicts rank 6, but a 2-cochain on a group of order eight is a function G² → M with 2^(8²) = 2^64 values, so DIRECT is impossible and I report 6 as <strong>ring-prediction-only</strong>, marked as such in fig27. The staircase 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, — is the replacement for the §9 sawtooth: where a cyclic frame-group offered a single bit (“is there a load-bearing remainder, yes or no”), a frame-group with k independent directions offers a whole degree-two space of them, one new family of independent generative diagonals for each <em>pair</em> of frame-directions (the cross-terms x_i x_j) on top of one per direction (the squares).</p><p>The odd-prime case is worth one honest sentence. For an odd prime p the ring <code>H*((Zp)^k, Fp)</code> is an exterior algebra on degree-one generators tensored with a polynomial algebra on degree-two generators, a genuinely different ring; but its <em>degree-two dimension still equals k(k+1)/2</em> (k from the polynomial generators, C(k,2) from products of the exterior generators), so the staircase is the same integer sequence for every prime, arrived at through different algebra. I flag this as a ring fact I am <em>using</em>, DIRECT-verified only at p = 2; I do not re-derive it and I do not enumerate the odd-p non-cyclic case (the same 2^64-type wall).</p><h3>12.3 Law L: the module multiplies the core — one independent copy per generator <em>(figures/fig28_law_L.png)</em></h3><p><strong>Verified-on-a-family, with the capped cases marked prediction-only.</strong> Combining the two axes — frame-group shape and module rank — the trivial-action rank obeys a product law: <code>rank H2((Z2)^k, (Z2)^r) = r &middot; C(k+1,2)</code>, i.e. each of the r independent F₂-generators of the value module contributes its own independent copy of the shape-determined core. DIRECT enumeration confirms every case the cap allowed: for the cyclic frame-group G = Z₂ the ranks are exactly r (core 1) for r = 1, 2, 3; for the non-cyclic G = Z₂×Z₂ the rank is 3 for r = 1 (core 3). The remaining points on the G = Z₂×Z₂ line (r = 2 predicting rank 6, r = 3 predicting rank 9) need 4^16 and larger cochain spaces, so they are drawn hollow in fig28 and reported as <strong>ring-prediction-only</strong>. The law itself is the additivity of group cohomology in the coefficient module for trivial coefficients (H²(G, A⊕B) = H²(G,A) ⊕ H²(G,B)); I verify its arithmetic consequence rather than re-prove the additivity.</p><h3>12.4 The cap frontier, stated honestly — non-trivial actions on non-cyclic groups</h3><p><strong>Cannot-claim, by design.</strong> Two boundaries are worth naming precisely rather than blurring. First, over M = Z₂ the automorphism group is trivial, so <em>no</em> non-trivial action of any frame-group exists at all — the trivial-action numbers in §§12.1—12.3 are the whole story for that module, not a special case of it. Second, the smallest module admitting a non-trivial non-cyclic action, M = Z₂×Z₂ under G = Z₂×Z₂ (with Aut = GL(2, F₂), order six), has a 2-cochain space of size 4^16 ≈ 4.3 billion, so DIRECT is impossible and no non-trivial-action number for a non-cyclic frame-group is claimed anywhere in this project. This is the exact generalization of the enumeration blow-up avoided since §8, and I record it as a hard limit rather than paper over it: the non-trivial-action phase law for non-cyclic frame-groups is <strong>open</strong>, and closing it would require the full Lyndon—Hochschild—Serre differentials, not a bigger computer.</p><h3>12.5 What §12 settles, and the one-line upgrade</h3><p>§12 converts the §11 break from an anecdote into a law with a boundary. The proven core is the elementary-abelian staircase <code>dim H2((Z2)^k, F2) = C(k+1,2)</code> together with the product law <code>rank H2((Z2)^k,(Z2)^r) = r&middot;C(k+1,2)</code>, DIRECT-verified wherever the cap allowed and ring-predicted (and labelled as such) beyond it. The single sharpest fact is the §12.1 refutation: at fixed order four, Z₄ gives rank 1 and Z₂×Z₂ gives rank 3, so the generative rank is <strong>not</strong> a function of |G| — it is a function of the group’s shape, and the §9 single-invariant law was a cyclic-group artifact in the strongest possible sense (two groups of the same order and the same 2-adic valuation disagree). The governance reading, flagged as analogy throughout exactly as in every prior section: a system whose meta-tiers come in a <em>bundle</em> of independent frame-directions (a non-cyclic frame-group) can host a strictly richer family of load-bearing paradoxes than a system whose tiers stack in a single line (a cyclic one) — and the count is combinatorial in the number of independent directions, C(k+1,2), not linear in the height of the stack. Frames-in-a-bundle carry more irreducible paradox than frames-in-a-line, and the cohomology ring says exactly how much. What stays open is honest and specific: every non-trivial action on a non-cyclic frame-group (the 4^16 wall), the odd-prime non-cyclic ranks (verified only through the ring, not enumeration), and — inherited from §11 — the characterization of which 2-groups admit a fully-collapsing action. The math is a law now, with its edges marked; the bridge to institutions is still the same unbuilt bridge.</p>
<hr><h2>13. Non-trivial actions on a non-cyclic frame group — past the 4¹⁶ wall by an explicit resolution <em>(cohomology_resolution.py, cohomology_resolution_run.py; figures/fig29_engine_validation.png, fig30_nontrivial_action_spectrum.png)</em></h2><p>§12 closed the non-cyclic frame-group law for the <em>trivial</em> action and marked the non-trivial case explicitly <strong>open</strong>, behind a hard computational wall: for G = Z₂×Z₂ acting non-trivially on a module M, a 2-cochain is a function G² → M, so direct enumeration costs |M|^16 (4¹⁶ ≈ 4.3 billion already at |M| = 4). This section gets past that wall — not with a bigger computer, but with a better object. First, a note recorded in answer to the standing side-question of whether the Lyndon—Hochschild—Serre (LHS) spectral sequence is the only route: it is not. There are at least four (direct enumeration, blocked; LHS, valid but carrying hidden d² differentials and extension problems for a non-trivial action; an explicit free resolution of ℤ over ℤ[G]; and a minimal F²-resolution, clean only for trivial coefficients). I use the <strong>explicit ℤ[G]-resolution</strong> as the engine, because every number it yields is the invariant-factor decomposition of an explicit integer matrix — verifiable, exact, and cheap (polynomial in |M|) — and I use LHS and a second independent enumeration only as cross-checks on the trivial action before trusting the engine on the non-trivial numbers it alone can reach.</p><h3>13.1 The engine, and why it is trusted <em>(cohomology_resolution.py)</em></h3><p><strong>Literal, and self-checking.</strong> For a single Z²... generator the standard resolution of ℤ over ℤ[Z₂] is 2-periodic, with differentials alternating multiplication by (s−1) and by the norm (s+1). For G = Z₂×Z₂ = ⟨s⟩×⟨t⟩ I build the <em>tensor product</em> of the two one-generator resolutions (the total complex of the double complex, with the Koszul sign rule), giving explicit free ℤ[G]-modules P₀ ← P₁ ← P₂ ← P₃ whose differentials are integer block matrices. The code <em>verifies this is a genuine complex</em> — d² = 0 and εd₁ = 0 hold exactly on the integer matrices — so H² = ker(δ₂)/im(δ₁) is honest group cohomology. Applying Hom_{ℤ[G]}(−, M) turns the resolution into a cochain complex of ℤ-linear maps twisted by the module action; because the cochain groups are tiny (|M|^{≤4} ≤ 16⁴), I compute ker and im by exact enumeration over the finite cochain groups and read off invariant factors by primary decomposition. No floating point, no sampling.</p><p><strong>Validation (the part that earns trust).</strong> On the trivial action the engine must reproduce numbers already known by other means. It does, and I checked them three ways: the explicit resolution, the LHS/Künneth associated-graded, and a fully independent bar-resolution enumeration all give |H²(Z₂×Z₂, Z₂)| = 8 = (Z₂)³, |H²(Z₂×Z₂, Z₄)| = 8, and |H²(Z₂×Z₂, Z₂×Z₂)| = 64. Three independent methods, one answer (fig29). The first of these is exactly the DIRECT=LHS rank-three number from §§11—12; the engine agreeing with it is the licence to believe the engine where DIRECT cannot follow. (One honest bug was caught and fixed in passing: my first action-classifier mislabelled which enumerated pair was the identity on the mixed module Z₂×Z₄, reporting its trivial baseline as rank 3 instead of the correct rank 6; the genuine-identity check fixed it, and the corrected baseline agrees with LHS. The <em>spectrum</em> of values was never wrong, only the label on the baseline.)</p><h3>13.2 The new numbers: what a non-trivial action does to the generative core <em>(figures/fig30_nontrivial_action_spectrum.png)</em></h3><p><strong>Verified-on-a-family (exact, first computed here).</strong> With the engine trusted, I enumerate <em>every</em> genuine Z₂×Z₂ action on each module — every commuting pair (S, T) of order-≤2 module automorphisms — and compute H² for each. These are the numbers the 4¹⁶ wall had put strictly out of reach; to my knowledge nothing earlier in the project computed a single non-trivial non-cyclic-frame value. The rank spectra:</p><table border="1" cellpadding="6" cellspacing="0" class="e-rte-table"> <tbody><tr><th>Module M</th><th>trivial-action rank</th><th>rank over all actions</th><th>reading</th></tr><tr><td>Z₄</td><td>3</td><td><strong>3 always</strong> (all 4 actions)</td><td>rigid: no order-2 action moves the core at all</td></tr><tr><td>Z₂×Z₂</td><td>6</td><td>1 or 6 (9 of 10 actions give 1)</td><td>brittle: almost every non-trivial action collapses rank 6 → 1</td></tr><tr><td>Z₂×Z₄</td><td>6</td><td>3, 4, or 6 (floor 3)</td><td>partial: drops to 4 or 3 but never below an irreducible floor of 3</td></tr><tr><td>Z₄×Z₄</td><td>6</td><td>full ladder 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 (400 actions)</td><td>the whole rank ladder is realized by non-cyclic actions</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Two facts hold across all four modules and are worth stating as findings rather than examples. First, <strong>the trivial action maximizes the rank</strong>: in every module the largest rank over all actions equals the trivial-action rank, and <em>no</em> non-trivial action ever exceeds it. A frame-bundle that acts on the value grain can only <em>reduce</em> the irreducible-paradox count relative to a frame-bundle that leaves it fixed; acting never manufactures new load-bearing diagonals. This is the exact non-cyclic-frame analogue of the §11 result that the trivial action maximizes the rank for a cyclic frame-group, now verified for a genuinely non-cyclic G past the enumeration wall. Second, <strong>how far the action can drop the rank is module-dependent, and there is a floor</strong> — Z₄ cannot be moved from 3 at all, Z₂×Z₄ has an irreducible floor of 3 that no action can breach, while Z₂×Z₂ and Z₄×Z₄ can be driven all the way to rank 1. The floor is the non-cyclic-frame refinement of §11’s refuted-collapse story: even with the full two-dimensional frame-bundle acting, some modules retain an irreducible generative remainder that no reachable frame — cyclic or non-cyclic, trivial or acting — can gauge away.</p><h3>13.3 What §13 settles, and the seams</h3><p>§13 does one clean thing: it removes the wall that §12 left standing, and it does so honestly, by replacing an infeasible enumeration with an exact resolution whose correctness is cross-checked three ways before it is trusted. The proven part is the engine itself — the resolution is verified to be a complex and reproduces two independent methods on the trivial action. The verified-on-a-family part is the new table: the complete H² spectrum of Z₂×Z₂ acting on Z₄, Z₂×Z₂, Z₂×Z₄, and Z₄×Z₄ over every commuting order-≤2 action, with the two structural findings (trivial action maximizes rank; module-dependent floor). What stays <strong>open</strong> is specific and honestly bounded: the engine is built for Z₂×Z₂ and a different frame-group needs its own resolution; and I have exact values on a family of modules, not a closed formula for H²(Z₂×Z₂, M, action) over all finite M and all actions (the ladder for Z₄×Z₄ is suggestive but not a theorem). The governance reading, flagged as analogy throughout every section and unchanged here: a system whose oversight tiers form a genuine two-dimensional bundle and whose two directions <em>act</em> on the value grain (rather than sitting inertly beside it) can only ever <em>reduce</em> the number of independent load-bearing paradoxes it carries, never increase it beyond what an inert bundle would hold — and against certain value grains a hard core of irreducible paradox survives even the full acting bundle. The math has reached past the wall it set itself; the bridge to institutions is, as ever, still unbuilt.</p>
<hr><h2>14. Bigger and mixed frame-bundles, and the collapse invariant &mdash; do the two &sect;13 facts survive, and what actually kills the generative core <em>(cohomology_bigger.py, cohomology_bigger_run.py, cohomology_collapse.py; figures/fig31_bigger_and_collapse.png, fig32_frame_torsion_injection.png)</em></h2><p>&sect;13 built an explicit &#8484;[G]-resolution engine for exactly one non-cyclic frame group, G = Z&#8322;&times;Z&#8322;, and read two structural facts off its complete non-trivial-action spectrum: the trivial action maximizes the rank, and each module keeps a floor of irreducible generative diagonals no reachable action can gauge away. Two questions were left explicitly open. First, are those two facts artifacts of the single group Z&#8322;&times;Z&#8322;, or do they survive a genuinely bigger and a genuinely mixed frame-bundle? P3 alone accounts for 7 of 8; P3-or-&sect;9-deep accounts for all 8.</p><h3>14.5 What &sect;14 settles, and the seams</h3><p>&sect;14 does two clean things and leaves the boundary honestly marked.
